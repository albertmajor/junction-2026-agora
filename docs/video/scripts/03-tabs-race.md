# 3. PriceMonkey: The camera that says $699

> A student in Helsinki looks for a used Sony A7 III across 20 tabs and a calculator. PriceMonkey reads the shop pages through Context.dev, throws out the wrong camera, puts a warning on the €239 bait and shows that the "cheap" US $699.95 is really €795. The pick turns out to be tab one, which was there all along.

Avg judge score 6.8 · 267 words · ~109 s

## Judge verdicts
- 6.5: It has the best cold open of the set, a problem-first thesis in two short lines. "A hundred sixty-one euros of import tax the listing never mentions" is the sharpest single receipt in any draft, and the tab-one callback with "without opening the other nineteen" makes the cost of the tab hunt land. But it slides into slop. "Exchange rate, import tax, calculator" is a list of three. "Nine euros a month, against twenty" plus the resale number is v1-style number cramming with no reason to care. It calls import VAT "import tax". There are lots of face-cam cuts for single words ("Sure."). "I'm a student in Helsinki" is an unverified credential. The money line ("a shop pays a cut, never a better spot") is good. Context.dev is said once and captioned, but nothing on screen shows it reading a page. Fix those and it's a contender.
- 7: It has the best hook of the five ("This camera says six ninety-nine. It doesn't cost that.") and the most pointed receipt: €161.50 the listing never mentions is aimed at a specific practice, not a vague villain. Every number checks out: 23 pages set aside, €795, €9.16 vs €20, resale around €715. It is the only script that makes cost per month after resale land as a reason, and that is PriceMonkey's most original feature. "I just couldn't know without opening the other nineteen" states the thesis without moralising. The weak spots are density and Context.dev. The pick beat (seven fifteen, nine a month, against twenty) is close to the v1 problem of piling up numbers. "Exchange rate, import tax, calculator" is a list of three. The voice-over calls import VAT "import tax". Context.dev gets one spoken line and a caption, but no on-screen moment where you can see it reading a page. Solid and safe, but it doesn't make the minimum requirement as vivid as the calculator script does.
- 6.5: The first four seconds are the best hook in the set, and the "There are twenty" crushed tab bar is a good visual. "I just couldn't know without opening the other nineteen" justifies the product better than Tab One's ending does. Then it gets too busy. "Twenty-three pages get thrown out" is a stat with no story behind it. "A hundred sixty-one euros of import tax", then "seven fifteen", then "nine euros a month against twenty" is exactly the number-cramming the bible warns about. By the pick I'm doing math instead of caring. "Emailing a shop for permission to pay them" is good, but it's just a line here, where Calculator actually sends the email on camera. The affiliate line, "a shop pays a cut, never a better spot", is clunky to say. It's competent, it's the most crowded of the five, and the 2x replay plus the four-part Context.dev disclosure make it feel like it's ticking off judging criteria.
- 7: It has the best cold open of the five and the best receipt line, 'A hundred sixty-one euros of import tax the listing never mentions', which is the mechanism read out loud. The crushed tab bar from 'Open All' is a cheap, legible visual for '2 of 20'. It loses points in three places. 'Exchange rate, import tax, calculator' is a banned list of three. The pick beat stacks seven fifteen, nine a month and twenty in eleven seconds, close to v1's number dump, and 'I resell it here' is a hypothetical said as fact. 'I'm a student in Helsinki' is a personal claim nobody has checked; it has to be true for whoever is on camera. Cutting to the face just for 'Sure.' costs an extra setup change for a two-frame joke. It works, but it is busier and harder to cut down than Tab One, which covers the same ground with less.
